# Experimental checks of the neural simulation

These are comparisons with published experiments on real cells, not just with other simulations. `tests/physiology.cpp` applies each stated protocol to the same neuron and synapse functions used by the GPU world. The full paper links below lead to the researchers' original reports.

## 1. Response to injected current

**Experiment.** [Naud, Marcille, Clopath and Gerstner, 2008, *Biological Cybernetics*](https://lcnwww.epfl.ch/gerstner/PUBLICATIONS/Naud08.pdf), Figure 8 and Table 1, recorded cortical cells under step-current injection and fitted the adaptive exponential integrate-and-fire (AdEx) model to individual cells. Figure 8 includes a regular-spiking pyramidal cell (RS, 130 pA) and a continuously non-adapting inhibitory cell (cNA, 150 pA).

**Simulation.** Table 1's nine fitted cell parameters are used directly for each profile. At the currents stated in Figure 8's experimental caption, a 2-second step produced 32 spikes in RS (130 pA), with first and last interspike intervals of 17 and 64 ms; cNA (150 pA) produced 73 spikes, with intervals of 23 and 27 ms. Table 1 lists different fitted *model* currents, 98 and 184 pA, at which our model emitted 22 and 100 spikes respectively. RS remained silent at zero injected current. Four 0.25 ms integration substeps per neural tick improved cNA spike count convergence at 150 pA: a one-substep version gave 69 spikes, four substeps 73, and a 0.1 ms numerical reference 74.

**Interpretation.** The qualitative adapting/non-adapting firing patterns are reproduced. This is a check of an implementation against the original paper's *fitted model and recorded pattern*, not an independent fit to raw experimental traces. The paper does not supply the underlying voltage samples here, so exact spike-time error against the recorded neurons has not been measured. Action-potential shape is intentionally replaced by a reset.

## 2. Paired-pulse synaptic depression

**Experiment.** [Tsodyks and Markram, 1997, *PNAS*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC19580/) used patch-clamp recordings from connected neocortical pyramidal neurons and fitted a synaptic-resource model. One reported fit uses utilization `U = 0.55` and recovery time `450 ms`.

**Simulation.** With those fitted values, a second presynaptic spike 50 ms after the first released a mean 0.279 of unit synaptic strength across 10,000 independent trials, versus 0.548 on the first (ratio 0.509). After a further 2 seconds, the mean response recovered to 0.546. The simulation gives each excitatory synapse its own resource state.

**Interpretation.** The depression and recovery direction follows the paired-recording result. These numerical outputs are predictions of the fitted model; they have not been independently compared against digitized EPSP amplitudes from the experiment. Individual trials now include quantal release and failures.

## 3. Spike-timing-dependent change

**Experiment.** [Bi and Poo, 1998, *Journal of Neuroscience*](https://www.gatsby.ucl.ac.uk/~pel/course_wuhan/papers/bi.poo_1998.pdf) made simultaneous recordings from connected cultured rat hippocampal neurons. Their 60 pairings at 1 Hz showed potentiation when a postsynaptic spike followed excitatory input within about 20 ms, depression for the reverse order, little change outside that window, and no significant change at glutamatergic inputs to GABAergic postsynaptic cells. Their reported mean potentiation in one comparison was 48.4% ± 9.9% SEM and depression was about 18%.

**Simulation.** Sixty +10 ms pairings raised a normalized weight from 1 to 1.415; sixty −10 ms pairings lowered it to 0.836. ±50 ms pairings and +10 ms pairing onto an inhibitory target left it at 1. The timing rule uses *presynaptic arrival* at the synapse rather than presynaptic spike emission.

**Interpretation.** The timing window and sign agree with the experiment, while the magnitudes were **calibrated** to its reported group means. They are therefore not independent validation. The original study also found dependence on initial synaptic strength and NMDA receptors; the model approximates strength dependence but has no explicit receptor or calcium dynamics. Its behaviour near zero time difference, for complex spike trains, and across other species or synapse classes is untested. This local rule should not be read as a universal biological learning rule.

## 4. Inhibitory current

Paired recordings report an approximately −75 mV inhibitory reversal potential ([Tarczy-Hornoch et al., 1998](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2230896/)); recordings of fast GABA-A currents support a decay on the order of 10 ms ([Hardie and Pearce, 2006](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6674351/)). The protocol check adds 4 nS of inhibitory conductance to a resting cNA cell, verifies hyperpolarization, then verifies that conductance falls to between 1 and 2 nS after 10 ms. These are limited checks of sign and timescale, not a recreation of either full recording protocol.

## Changes driven by these comparisons

The initial simulator used a coarse 5 ms integrate-and-fire tick, ad hoc activation/reset parameters, and globally released chemicals that directly changed eligible synapses. It now uses published AdEx cell fits, 1 ms spike timing with converged 0.25 ms voltage integration, conductance-based input, short-term release depletion, and a source-specific timing rule. The global chemical rule was removed because its particular effects and constants lacked a matching experimental protocol. No direct reward signal enters neural plasticity.

The environment's sensor-current scale, food chemistry, motor mapping, mutation ranges, and reproduction rules are artificial-world design choices, not claims about animal physiology. The current evidence supports the possibility of local activity-dependent synaptic change in this model. It does **not** establish that evolving animals will develop useful memory, adaptive learning, instincts, or ant-like behaviour.

## 5. Thin-dendrite summation

**Experiment.** [Polsky, Mel and Schiller, 2004, *Nature Neuroscience*](https://neurophysics.ucsd.edu/courses/physics_171/49528.pdf) used confocal imaging, focal stimulation at two identified sites, and somatic recordings from rat pyramidal neurons. Weak nearby inputs on one branch summed nearly linearly, intermediate inputs could sum superlinearly, strong inputs saturated, and inputs on different branches summed approximately linearly.

**Simulation.** Excitatory pyramidal cells now have four independently addressed dendritic compartments. A phenomenological sigmoid transforms each branch's decaying conductance before the branches combine at the soma. At weak two-site input, same-branch and separate-branch peak responses were 1.446 and 1.445 mV; at intermediate input, 8.695 and 6.943 mV; at strong input, 22.324 and 25.792 mV. The GPU genome assigns each excitatory synapse a mutable branch.

**Interpretation.** This reproduces the qualitative within- versus between-branch summation pattern. Branch count, conductance threshold, gain, and saturation were chosen for the protocol; they are not experimentally identified channel parameters. No explicit dendritic membrane voltage, calcium signal, NMDA receptor kinetics, or spatial distance within a branch is simulated. The original study observed failures and variability that this stage does not reproduce.

## 6. Facilitation and depression from one axon

**Experiment.** [Markram, Wang and Tsodyks, 1998, *PNAS*](https://web.stanford.edu/class/cs379c/archive/2016/class_messages_listing/articles/MarkrametalPNAS-98.pdf) simultaneously recorded a pyramidal neuron and two of its targets. During a 30 Hz six-spike train, its connection to another pyramidal neuron depressed while its connection to an interneuron facilitated. The paper gives a fitted facilitating connection with first-spike utilization 0.1, recovery time 30 ms, and facilitation time 1700 ms (Figure 3), and states the pulse-driven utilization equations.

**Simulation.** Each excitatory synapse now has its own running utilization and its own inherited facilitation time. The same six-spike schedule yielded mean sixth/first release ratios of 3.741 for the paper's facilitating profile and 0.134 for our existing depressing profile across 10,000 independent trials. Newly generated excitatory-to-inhibitory synapses start with the published facilitating profile, and their parameters may mutate. The revised CUDA world completed a 300-step run with births.

**Interpretation.** The response direction and target-specific difference are reproduced. The reported ratios are model outputs, not measured values transcribed from the paper; the experiment's voltage traces have not been digitized for an independent amplitude comparison. Individual release is now stochastic, while the expected response follows the paper's phenomenological fit.

## 7. Cortical plasticity needs depolarization and NMDA receptors

**Experiment.** [Sjöström, Turrigiano and Nelson, 2001, *Neuron*](https://plasticity.muhc.mcgill.ca/DataPage/PDFs/Sjostrom_Neuron_2001.pdf) recorded connected layer-5 pyramidal neurons in rat visual cortex. Fifty +10 ms spike pairs at 0.1 Hz failed to potentiate a weak unitary connection. Cooperating inputs or injected depolarization rescued potentiation; 40 Hz bursts were normally effective. Reverse timing produced depression at low rate. NMDA receptor blockade prevented the measured changes. A fitted half-maximum for depolarization-dependent potentiation was 2.1 mV, with a statistical threshold near 2.3 mV.

**Simulation.** Synapses now distinguish two experimentally studied plasticity profiles: the earlier cultured hippocampal protocol and this cortical layer-5 protocol. In the cortical profile, LTP requires recent postsynaptic depolarization of at least 2.3 mV; LTD remains timing-dependent. A 50-pair protocol gave normalized weights of 1.000 for isolated low-rate +10 ms pairs, 1.360 with 3 mV preceding depolarization, 0.861 for −10 ms pairs, and 1.000 when NMDA receptors were blocked in the experimental condition. In an integrated cell-and-synapse protocol, 15 bursts of five +10 ms pairs at 40 Hz, repeated every 10 seconds, produced 75 postsynaptic spikes **without background current**. The mean voltage just before spikes 2–5 was 7.24 mV above rest, close to the 7.3 mV example trace in Figure 5D, and normalized synaptic weight rose to 1.297. Fifty sparse pairs at 0.1 Hz left weight at 1.000. An afterpotential-disabled comparison, with stronger brief spike-evoking pulses to preserve all 75 postsynaptic spikes, gave −15.85 mV between spikes and weight 0.792. The GPU world uses the cortical profile initially and computes a short running estimate of preceding somatic depolarization.

**Interpretation.** The low-rate failure, depolarization rescue, reverse-timing depression, and pharmacological blockade are reproduced qualitatively. The 2.3 mV threshold comes from this experiment; the simulator's 10 ms voltage averaging is an approximation. The 40 Hz result now relies on a fitted spike afterpotential rather than injected background depolarization. Its 7.24 mV matches **one example cell**, not an independently held-out population mean; its weight change remains below the study's 153% ± 14% group mean. NMDA blockade is a protocol switch, not a simulated receptor or calcium process. Dopamine-dependent plasticity reported for other cell classes remains outside this cortical profile and is not yet reproduced.

## 7a. Post-spike afterdepolarization in cortical pyramidal cells

**Experiment.** [Haj-Dahmane and Andrade, 1997, *Journal of Neurophysiology*](https://journals.physiology.org/doi/10.1152/jn.1997.78.4.1983) recorded layer-5 rat prefrontal pyramidal cells. A single action potential was followed by a fast afterhyperpolarization and then a depolarizing afterpotential; calcium removal or buffering suppressed the latter. The inward aftercurrent after a calcium spike decayed by more than 90% within about 100–200 ms at room temperature. [Sjöström et al., 2001](https://plasticity.muhc.mcgill.ca/DataPage/PDFs/Sjostrom_Neuron_2001.pdf) observed incomplete repolarization during 40 Hz trains in rat visual-cortex layer-5 cells; Figure 5D gives an example residual depolarization of 7.3 mV.

**Simulation.** A separate cortical layer-5 profile retains the Naud regular-spiking membrane parameters but sets the post-spike reset to −68 mV and adds an inward aftercurrent of 120 pA per spike decaying with a 45 ms time constant. An isolated simulated spike gave relative voltages of −1.78, +1.61, +3.51, and +0.46 mV at 2, 10, 20, and 80 ms after the spike, respectively, without a second spike. The integrated 40 Hz voltage and plasticity outputs are reported above. Both aftercurrent parameters and the reset voltage were **calibrated** to the qualitative afterpotential and the 7.3 mV example, not measured directly in the visual-cortex cells. The new parameters are inherited and mutable in the GPU world.

**Interpretation.** The early hyperpolarization, delayed depolarization, and decay reproduce the observed order of events. The calcium-dependent cation current is one experimentally supported candidate mechanism in prefrontal neurons, but the simulation represents only its net current and does not claim the same molecular cause in visual cortex. The model does not simulate calcium, channel gating, the full action-potential waveform, or cell-to-cell variation. Its timescale is plausible from the cortical recording, while the exact voltage waveform still needs comparison with digitized experimental traces.

## 8. Variable release and failures

**Experiment.** In [Markram, Wang and Tsodyks, 1998, *PNAS*](https://web.stanford.edu/class/cs379c/archive/2016/class_messages_listing/articles/MarkrametalPNAS-98.pdf), simultaneous recordings from one pyramidal axon and two target cells reported first-response failures in 24% of trials at its interneuron connection and none in 60 trials at its pyramidal connection (Figure 2). Quantal analysis of a small identified sensory-to-motor circuit in [Castellucci and Kandel, 1974, *PNAS*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/4373738/) also supports release in discrete units rather than a perfectly continuous amount.

**Simulation.** Each excitatory connection now has a mutable count of independent effective release sites, each with a ready/depleted state. At a presynaptic arrival, ready sites release probabilistically, become depleted, and recover probabilistically. Fourteen effective sites with the two previously fitted utilization profiles gave first-response failure rates of 22.28% and 0% across 10,000 trials, while retaining the measured directions of facilitation and depression. The GPU gives each synapse its own random state; animal RNG is not shared during spike delivery.

**Interpretation.** This reproduces the failure-rate contrast and produces trial-to-trial quantal variability. The effective site count was **calibrated** to the reported failure probability; it is not an anatomical contact count. We have not compared the full amplitude distribution or coefficient of variation with the experiment. The model also omits spontaneous miniature events and variability in quantum size.

## 9. Slow homeostatic synaptic scaling

**Experiment.** [Turrigiano, Leslie, Desai, Rutherford and Nelson, 1998, *Nature*](https://faculty.washington.edu/somurray/psych545/Turrigiano_Nature_1998.pdf) measured miniature AMPA currents in cultured rat visual-cortex neurons after 48 hours of altered activity. Blocking spikes with TTX increased mean amplitude to 192% ± 16% of control. Blocking GABA-A inhibition with bicuculline initially raised firing to about 265% of control and reduced mean amplitude to 70% ± 4% after 48 hours. Changes were approximately multiplicative across excitatory inputs, developed gradually, and were not significant in bipolar interneurons under activity blockade. The study also found little change in mini-current kinetics or frequency.

**Simulation.** Excitatory pyramidal neurons now maintain a separate *postsynaptic* AMPA scale shared by their excitatory inputs. Its slow empirical response is calibrated to the study's 48-hour endpoints, with 0.4 Hz as the control culture rate. An accelerated protocol that advances the scaling rule one hour at a time gives factors of 1.92 for a continuously silent cell, 1.00 at control firing, 0.70 for a cell held at 2.65 times control firing, and 1.00 for an inhibitory cell. Multiplying two unequal inputs by the same factor preserves their relative strength. The full GPU world completed 2,000 steps and exported finite AMPA scales.

**Interpretation.** The direction, cell-type distinction, multiplicative property, and calibrated endpoints are reproduced. The paper did not report a fixed high firing rate throughout bicuculline treatment; its rate fell during adaptation, whereas the high-rate protocol above holds rate constant. The interpolation over time and between activity conditions is therefore a model assumption, not independently validated physiology. The 2,000-step world run spans only 32 seconds of neural time, so its scales remain near 1 (0.999934–1.00012). Testing the actual 48-hour cellular response requires the accelerated isolated protocol or a much longer neural simulation.

## 10. Delayed monoamine conversion of cortical eligibility traces

**Experiment.** [He et al., 2015, *Neuron*](https://escholarship.org/content/qt290403p6/qt290403p6_noSplash_15961229581c4dab94718b9699f4df0e.pdf) recorded two independent layer-IV to layer-II/III pathways in mouse visual cortex. Two hundred pairings at 10 Hz put one pathway 10 ms before a four-spike postsynaptic burst and the other 10 ms after it. Pairing alone gave no lasting synaptic change. A 10-second 50 μM noradrenaline puff immediately afterward raised the pre-before-post response to 132.3% ± 9.0% of baseline; serotonin lowered the post-before-pre response to 73.1% ± 4.5%. Neither modulator alone changed an unconditioned pathway. The LTP eligibility trace was roughly half reduced with a 5-second delay and no longer significant at 10 seconds; the LTD trace was no longer significant at 5 seconds. The authors also evoked endogenous transmitter release optogenetically: release after pairing changed the appropriately active pathway, while release before pairing did not (Figure 3). Blocking β2-adrenergic or 5-HT2C receptors prevented the respective conversion (Figure 5).

**Simulation.** A separate, heritable synapse phenotype stores silent LTP and LTD eligibility states with fitted 7.2- and 3.6-second decay constants. Noradrenaline or serotonin events convert the matching state only when that synapse has the corresponding receptor. The shared plasticity function applied to the two-pathway protocol produced 1.000/1.000 after pairing alone, 1.322/1.000 with noradrenaline, and 1.000/0.732 with serotonin (pre-before-post/post-before-pre). Delaying noradrenaline by 10 seconds gave 1.080; delaying serotonin by 5 seconds gave 0.933. Modulator alone or a missing matching receptor left weight at 1.000. A 300-step CUDA world run completed with births; hidden neurons can inherit and mutate a modulatory transmitter, and connections can inherit and mutate receptor availability and this plasticity phenotype. Modulator pulses are emitted by neurons through ordinary connections, not by the environment as rewards.

**Interpretation.** The immediate amplitudes and trace decay constants were **calibrated** to this study; agreement at those conditions is a reproduction of the fitted protocol, not independent validation. The test uses controlled transmitter application, as in the experiment, whereas world release follows spikes in evolving circuitry. The model represents a transient synaptic state and receptor-specific conversion, not the underlying kinase, calcium, cAMP, or receptor-binding kinetics. The experimental profile is available to evolution without requiring a cortical layer or any particular brain layout.

## 11. Dopamine-sensitive eligibility at striatal synapses

**Experiment.** [Shindou et al., 2019, *European Journal of Neuroscience*](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/ejn.13921) made whole-cell recordings from identified adult mouse striatal D1 and D2 projection neurons. Sixty pairings, one presynaptic EPSP 10 ms before a three-spike 100 Hz postsynaptic burst, were delivered 10 seconds apart. In D1 cells, pairing alone depressed the response by 32.3% ± 4.6%; the corresponding D2 group did not change significantly. Photolytic dopamine release at −2, 0, +2, or +4 seconds relative to pairing showed a narrow window. With nimodipine blocking competing L-type-calcium-dependent LTD, +2-second release produced 27.2% ± 8.81% LTP; the other tested delays did not produce significant LTP. The authors also observed a transient rectification change consistent with calcium-permeable AMPA receptors after pairing, and found that their blockade prevented LTP whereas NMDA antagonism did not.

**Simulation.** A distinct heritable synapse phenotype generates a silent dopamine-sensitive state after a pre-before-post conjunction and a competing L-type-dependent depression. The eligibility time course is a phenomenological rise-and-fall function fitted to peak near 2 seconds. The same shared plasticity code gave normalized responses of 0.677 after pairing alone and 1.000 with nimodipine but no dopamine. With nimodipine, dopamine at −2, 0, +2, and +4 seconds gave 1.000, 1.000, 1.272, and 1.080. Without nimodipine, +2-second dopamine gave 0.949, counteracting the otherwise observed LTD. Test switches reproduce the finding that D1 or calcium-permeable AMPA blockade prevents this LTP, whereas NMDA blockade does not. Dopamine-capable hidden neurons and matching receptor-bearing synapses are heritable and mutable in the CUDA world; a 300-step run completed with births.

**Interpretation.** This is a reproduction of the fitted synaptic timing and blocker outcomes, not an independent prediction. The simulation does not yet have a fitted medium spiny neuron membrane, a D2-specific comparison phenotype, explicit L-type channels, receptor kinetics, or measured calcium-permeable AMPA insertion; the pharmacological manipulations are protocol switches. The D1-like synapse phenotype can evolve anywhere in the graph and does not impose striatal anatomy. The +4-second simulated increase is within the broad experimental uncertainty and should be checked against additional timing points before treating the fitted envelope as a biological kinetic law.

## 12. Dopamine timing and single-spine structural plasticity

**Experiment.** [Yagishita et al., 2014, *Science*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25258080/) used glutamate uncaging at an individual spine of an identified D1-receptor medium spiny neuron, paired with postsynaptic activity, and optically stimulated dopamine fibers with ten 30 Hz pulses. Dopamine at 0.6 or 1 second after pairing onset significantly increased spine enlargement; stimulation 1 second before or 5 seconds after did not. The authors reported a roughly 0.3–2-second sensitive window. D1-receptor, NMDA-receptor, and PKA inhibitors reduced the enlargement. Neighboring unstimulated spines provided a locality check.

**Simulation.** An optional, heritable synapse phenotype stores a local structural eligibility age and a separate normalized spine-volume proxy. The isolated protocol applies ten pre/post pairs at 10 Hz and the reported ten-pulse 30 Hz dopamine train. Simulated volume was 1.000 with pairing alone, 1.000 with dopamine 1 second before, 1.216 at +0.6 seconds, 1.241 at +1 second, and 1.000 at +5 seconds. An unstimulated neighboring synapse remained 1.000 despite receiving the same dopamine events. Dopamine alone and simulated D1, NMDA, or PKA blockade also prevented growth. The CUDA world completed 300 steps with births; of 1,502 final synapses carrying this phenotype, five grew above 1.00001, with a maximum volume of 1.299.

**Interpretation.** The temporal window and effect size in this proxy were chosen to reproduce the reported qualitative timing; the original spine-volume traces were not digitized, so these amplitudes are **not** a numerical match to measured volumes. The experiment paired local glutamate uncaging and action potentials, while the simulation approximates that with synaptic arrivals and spikes; it does not simulate glutamate uncaging, spines as geometry, calcium, cAMP, PKA kinetics, or phosphodiesterase activity. Inhibitors are protocol switches. Crucially, spine volume is currently an **observed structural state only**: it does not alter synaptic conductance or animal behaviour. This check therefore adds evidence-grounded local structural plasticity but does not itself establish functional learning or memory. The phenotype can evolve at arbitrary connections without imposing nucleus accumbens anatomy.

## 13. Isolated lateral pyloric neuron current sweep

**Experiment.** [Nowotny, Levi and Selverston, 2008, *PLOS ONE*](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0002627) isolated six identified lobster LP neurons from their synaptic network and applied 20-second DC injections from approximately −4 to +2 nA. At zero injected current the cells fired irregularly with occasional hyperpolarizations. Strong hyperpolarization yielded shorter, more regular bursts; depolarizing current led toward tonic spiking. The authors fitted a two-compartment conductance model to recordings from one neuron and [published its C++ implementation](https://modeldb.science/116957).

**Simulation.** `src/lp_cell.cuh` implements that paper's fitted axonal and somatic current equations: fast sodium, delayed-rectifier potassium, two calcium currents, calcium-dependent potassium, A, h, M, and separate leaks. It uses the reported initial conditions and fitted parameter values, compensating the authors' 2 nA fitting offset. The shared neuron update calls this profile when an inherited hidden cell has type 2; its calcium, calcium-dependent potassium, h, and leak densities may mutate. `tests/pyloric_lp.cpp` applies 20-second current steps with the first five seconds discarded, as in the published protocol. For −4, 0 and +1 nA, it recorded 79, 109 and 121 axonal spikes over the subsequent 15 seconds. The longest spike-free intervals were 2,397, 1,529 and 399 ms, respectively. At +1 nA the soma spent no time below −65 mV. A 100-step GPU run contained 131 type-2 cells at its end, 65 active, and passed global genome accounting.

**Interpretation.** This reproduces the broad current-dependent transition using a **previously fitted model**, so agreement is not an independent prediction. We did not have digitized voltage traces from the six recorded cells and did not match their individual spike times, burst duration distributions, or variability. Fixed-step 0.05 ms integration and 1 ms spike readout can also differ from the authors' adaptive integration and high-resolution recordings. The isolated preparation retained chemical influences that were not all removed; this protocol does not prove completely autonomous LP firing. The optional LP profile can occur anywhere in an evolving graph and does not prescribe pyloric circuitry.

## 14. Pyloric pacemaker leak conductance: passive subexperiment

**Experiment.** [Zhao, Golowasch and Nadim, 2010, *Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience*](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/behavioral-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnbeh.2010.00021/full) measured PD neuron current while ramping membrane voltage from −60 to −80 mV. A representative PD neuron had an input conductance of 71 nS with descending neuromodulatory input and 149 nS after decentralization (Figure 4A). Across cells, the difference current reversed at −63.6 ± 9.5 mV. The same study restored network rhythm with a −80 nS dynamic-clamp leak in one AB or PD pacemaker neuron, but not in an LP follower (Figure 2). A positive-slope component of the modulatory current failed to restore rhythm (Figure 6).

**Simulation.** The shared neuron update now accepts an optional linear dynamic-clamp current with a signed conductance and specified reversal potential. `tests/pyloric_leak.cpp` performs paired steady-state current probes on a quiet 149 nS membrane. Without clamp it measures 149 nS; a −78 nS clamp yields 71 nS; a +78 nS control increases conductance to 227 nS. The zero-current membrane remains at −63.6 mV. These use the representative Figure 4 slopes to check units, sign, and reversibility of the current implementation.

**Interpretation.** This reproduces the **passive electrical effect** and its measured scale using parameters set from Figure 4. It is a calibrated protocol check, not an independent prediction or a reproduction of pacemaker rhythm. The evolving world has no separately identified AB and PD cells, electrical coupling between them, or endogenous pyloric modulator current. An isolated LP cell is a follower and cannot substitute for that pacemaker circuit. Figure 2 and Figure 6's rhythm and target-specific controls therefore remain open experimental comparisons; this section must not be cited as reproducing them.

## 15. Leak-dependent bursting in a published pyloric pacemaker model

**Source and protocol.** [Prinz, Bucher and Marder, 2004, *Nature Neuroscience*](https://www.nature.com/articles/nn1352) studied millions of three-neuron pyloric network models with conductance-based cells. The public [pyloric simulator](https://github.com/mackelab/pyloric) provides the PM_0 pacemaker conductances and equations used here. The [Zhao et al. 2010](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/behavioral-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnbeh.2010.00021/full) dynamic-clamp experiment motivated the intervention: increased input conductance after decentralization and an approximately −80 nS clamp that can restore bursting when applied to a pacemaker neuron.

**Simulation.** `src/prinz_cell.cuh` ports the published eight-current membrane model, intracellular calcium buffer, and voltage-dependent gates. It is an optional heritable type-3 cell in the GPU world with independently mutable channel conductances; no fixed circuit layout is imposed. Its 0.025 ms internal step detects spikes before the 1 ms world readout. `tests/pyloric_pacemaker.cpp` compares four 20-second conditions after a 10-second settling period: published PM_0 control, an added 80 nS leak at −68 mV, a −80 nS dynamic clamp that cancels that addition, and a +80 nS control. In the analysis window, the conditions yielded 112, 0, 112 and 0 spikes, respectively; control and rescue each had six gaps between bursts. The CUDA world ran 100 steps with 25 type-3 cells, including 12 active cells, and exported finite genome values.

**Interpretation.** This model-specific assay demonstrates that a published pacemaker cell can switch between bursting and silence through a leak-conductance change of the experimental order of magnitude. The added 80 nS leak was selected for this comparison, and the negative clamp directly cancels it, so restoration is a mechanistic check rather than an independent prediction of the experiment. The PM_0 model represents a **lumped AB/PD pacemaker**, not separate AB and PD neurons. We have not reproduced the observed rescue from stimulating one of those cells, the LP-target failure in a connected preparation, or the triphasic network phase. The world still uses spike-triggered connections rather than the graded pyloric chemical synapses and electrical coupling needed for that comparison.

## 16. Sensory relay: ORN to PN protocols (partial reproduction)

**Experiments.** [Kazama and Wilson, 2008, *Neuron*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2429849/) stimulated one identified fruit-fly olfactory receptor neuron (ORN) axon while recording a projection neuron (PN). Mean unitary current at sparse stimulation was 29.0 ± 2.6 pA (45 PNs); mean unitary voltage response was 6.19 ± 0.45 mV (23 PNs). These means come from different groups of cells. Unitary responses had a mean current coefficient of variation of 0.16. A 7 Hz baseline train depressed synaptic responses by about 40%; 20 and 50 Hz challenges depressed them further over 500 ms. [Supplementary Figure S8](https://kazamalab.riken.jp/pdf/Neuron_Kazama%26Wilson_2008_supplement.pdf) reports slow recovery after a 7 Hz train, with a fitted recovery time constant of 7.5 seconds. [Bhandawat and colleagues, 2007, *Nature Neuroscience*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2838615/) measured ORN and PN odor spike trains in matched glomeruli: weak ORN responses were amplified in PNs, and PN responses emphasized odor onset. They also found evidence of lateral inputs, so this is a circuit-level comparison rather than a single-synapse measurement.

**Published spatial reference.** [Gouwens and Wilson, 2009](https://wilson.hms.harvard.edu/sites/g/files/omnuum8421/files/wilson-lab/files/gouwenswilson2009.pdf) reconstructed a PN and fitted passive properties, showing that distributed ORN contacts on terminal dendrites matter. `tools/reproduce_orn_pn_reference.py` runs a 25-contact protocol in their public [ModelDB implementation](https://github.com/ModelDBRepository/118662), using its morphology and leak mechanism but NEURON `Exp2Syn` inputs. With a fixed random set of terminal dendrites and 0.18 nS/contact, it yields a 5.83 mV somatic EPSP and a 29.88 pA peak under somatic voltage clamp. Both lie in the reported mean ± SEM ranges, but the contact conductance is fitted to those observations and the two published means come from different cells. The author model is passive and cannot test PN firing. To rerun outside this repository, install NEURON, clone the ModelDB repository, compile its mechanisms with `nrnivmodl`, then invoke this script with the checkout path and `--mode current` or `--mode voltage`. The external model is not bundled here.

**CUDA-world assay.** `tests/sensory_relay.cpp` uses the same neuron-step and release functions as the GPU world. Its ORN-like connection has 50 sites, utilization 0.75, a 50 ms fast recovery time, and an optional 7.5 s slow resource that loses 2.3% per spike. These last two values are phenomenological fits to the 7 Hz depression and slow recovery observations; the experiment does not identify a unique molecular mechanism. The new type-4 PN relay profile uses the existing spiking membrane equation with an effective 8.7 pF capacitance and 12 ms leak time constant, fitted to the unitary EPSP and approximate decay of the published spatial reference. These are effective point-cell values, **not measured PN compartment properties**. Type 4 and the slow synaptic component are heritable and mutable, but no specific circuit layout is installed in founders. The 40-axon convergence challenge is a controlled test graph.

The 7 Hz baseline and each challenge last 4 s and 500 ms respectively; onset and late rates use 100 ms windows. The 20/50 Hz PN values are **qualitative** comparisons to the published odor experiment, which used living ORNs, PNs, lateral inputs, and different odors. Their exact rates were not fitted here. The evenly staggered 7 Hz input produces no spontaneous PN spikes; section 17 tests how this changes with independently timed ORN spikes. The trial-to-trial current CV is also too small. Its point-neuron EPSP decays more slowly over the first 20 ms than the spatial reference, even when its peak and later tail are close. Thus the single-axon amplitude, train depression, slow recovery, and stimulus-to-spike propagation are reproduced in controlled protocols, while full ORN-to-PN dynamics and in vivo odor responses remain **unreproduced**.

**World implication.** Zero-bias sensory ports do emit spikes at input levels 0.25 and above, but no spikes at 0.125 in the 500 ms assay. Founders currently inherit a 0–100 pA sensory bias, so their weak-input activity depends on that unvalidated current. With a 50 pA bias, the sensory port emits 3 spikes without input and 13 at level 0.125. The world has only 16 fixed sensory ports, fewer than the axons used in this convergence assay, and founder graphs need not converge onto a relay. The assay therefore diagnoses a missing membrane/connection regime without claiming that the current evolving world already exhibits the measured fly circuit.

## 17. Spontaneous ORN timing and shared PN input

**Experiment.** [Kazama and Wilson, 2009, *Nature Neuroscience*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2751859/) recorded spontaneous activity from identified fly PNs. They report that PNs typically fire at 1–5 spikes/s without odor and that PNs receiving input from the same ORN population have correlated spontaneous spikes. PNs in different glomeruli are weakly correlated. Paired recordings attribute most shared activity to common ORN input, while reciprocal PN connections make a smaller contribution. These observations motivate testing input timing and common versus separate presynaptic spike trains before adding any intrinsic PN drive.

**Controlled assay.** In `tests/sensory_relay.cpp`, 40 ORN-like synapses each receive a 7 Hz mean baseline followed by a 500 ms challenge. The baseline lasts 4 s; PN baseline rate is measured over its last 500 ms. Three timing protocols use exactly the same release and membrane functions: evenly staggered periodic spikes, periodic spikes with independent random phases, and independent Bernoulli events in 1 ms bins. The last is a timing **null model**, not a claim that measured ORN intervals are Poisson. Input and vesicle-release random streams are independently seeded. Results below average 200 trials; ± values are standard errors across trials.

| Input timing, 40 ORNs | PN baseline | PN at 20 Hz challenge | PN at 50 Hz challenge |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Evenly staggered periodic | 0 spikes/s | 60 spikes/s | 88 spikes/s |
| Independent random phase, periodic | 11.36 ± 0.31 spikes/s | 58.38 ± 0.19 spikes/s | 86.59 ± 0.13 spikes/s |
| Independent intervals | 4.44 ± 0.18 spikes/s | 38.22 ± 0.25 spikes/s | 71.22 ± 0.18 spikes/s |

At 50 Hz with independent intervals, the first and last 100 ms of PN output are 117.4 and 41.9 spikes/s. The sampled ORN rates are 7.02 and 49.89 spikes/s. The 4.44 spikes/s spontaneous PN rate falls in the reported 1–5 spikes/s range, **without a constant PN current or lateral circuit**. This is a result of independent ORN timing in the controlled 40-input circuit, not a validated fit to the full distribution of recorded ORN spike trains. The three timing conditions differ markedly despite matching mean presynaptic rates, so the earlier zero-baseline finding depended on the evenly staggered input protocol.

**Shared-input check.** Two simulated PNs with independent vesicle release receive either the same 40 ORN spike trains or separate trains, each at 7 Hz. Across 100 trials, their final-baseline rates are 5.02 and 4.82 spikes/s with shared input, versus 5.02 and 4.38 with separate input. Their 50 ms spike-count correlations are 0.861 and −0.010 respectively. This reproduces the **direction** of the paired-recording result. The paper used spike cross-correlations and found more modest correlations; these coefficients are not numerically comparable, and the test circuit omits other private and lateral inputs.

**World implication.** With independent timing, the same relay receives almost no baseline drive from only 16 ORNs: a 100-trial sweep gives 0, 0, 0.10, 4.32, and 25.52 PN spikes/s for 8, 16, 24, 40, and 64 afferents respectively. At a 20 Hz challenge those counts yield 0, 0.20, 5.70, 38.36, and 94.06 PN spikes/s. This is a sharp convergence dependence in this particular fitted phenotype. The evolving world has only 16 fixed sensory ports and does not supply independent 40-axon input to a hidden neuron, so this successful assay does **not** show that the world currently has enough sensory drive for bias-free relay firing. No world topology or sensory drive was changed by this experiment.
